## Overview

The injector plate for CU SRL's regeneratively cooled engine (see the
[engine project](/Portfolio/projects/regeneratively-cooled-engine) for
the chamber sizing this feeds into) is an unlike-doublet impinging
design with an integrated ASI (augmented spark igniter) port and
pressure sensor mounts. It is printed in Inconel by an outside vendor.
This page covers the injector work: the impingement math, the manifold
layout, the vendor's manufacturability review, the redesign it forced
for additive manufacturing, and the topology optimization pass that
shaped the final plate.

Most of the layout decisions started from NASA SP-8089, *Liquid Rocket
Engine Injectors*, the 1976 NASA Space Vehicle Design Criteria
monograph that's still the standard reference for impinging-injector
geometry, orifice sizing, and manifold design.

## Injector layout

**Math first.** Before modeling anything, I worked through the
impingement geometry math (orifice sizing, doublet impingement angle,
and momentum ratio between the fuel and oxidizer streams) to get
atomization and mixing characteristics that matched the mass flow rates
coming out of the chamber sizing work. I also weighed a radially
symmetric orifice pattern early on, but settled on the doublet layout
because it gave more direct control over the impingement angle and
momentum balance between propellants than a fully radial pattern would
have.

**Then CAD: a lot of it.** Getting from that math to an actual 3D
model took many iterations. Early passes focused just on getting flow
paths and manifolds to fit within the envelope; later passes folded in
manufacturability and structural constraints as they came up.

- Designed a stacked distribution ring and manifold on the oxidizer side to feed the doublet orifices evenly.
- Used a gothic-arch geometry on the fuel side, the same profile masonry has used for centuries to span an opening without exceeding an overhang or shear limit, to maximize usable space for preheated kerosene routing into the regenerative cooling channels while staying inside the 45° rule.

## The numbers behind the layout

All of the impingement and orifice sizing lives in one
[Desmos sheet](https://www.desmos.com/calculator/yzwcyebbm7). It is a
chain: the operating point sets the jet velocities, the velocities and
flows set the orifice areas, the momentum of each stream sets the
impingement angles, and the angles and diameters set the face and plate
geometry. These are the choices in that chain that shaped the plate.

<ul class="choices">
<li><span class="choice-title">One manifold pressure for both propellants</span>Both manifolds sit at <span class="choice-num">420 psi</span> against a <span class="choice-num">350 psi</span> chamber, a <span class="choice-num">70 psi</span> drop that is 20% of chamber pressure. That stiffness keeps chamber pressure swings from feeding back into the flow, and equal manifold pressure keeps the feed system simple.</li>
<li><span class="choice-title">Angles from a momentum balance</span>The angles aren't picked, they're solved. Oxidizer carries <span class="choice-num">70.8 N</span> of momentum and fuel <span class="choice-num">43.8 N</span>, so the ox jet leans <span class="choice-num">67.7°</span> and the fuel jet <span class="choice-num">52.3°</span> from the face. Their radial components cancel at <span class="choice-num">26.8 N</span> each, so each fan points straight down the chamber instead of into the wall.</li>
<li><span class="choice-title">24 pairs against a hole-size floor</span>More pairs means finer atomization but smaller holes, and every orifice is meant to stay above a <span class="choice-num">0.8 mm</span> floor. At 24 pairs the ox hole is <span class="choice-num">Ø1.97 mm</span> and the fuel hole <span class="choice-num">Ø1.55 mm</span>. The 24 film holes land at <span class="choice-num">Ø0.79 mm</span>, a hair under the floor, which makes them the feature to watch.</li>
<li><span class="choice-title">Impinge six diameters out</span>SP-8089 asks for 5 to 7 diameters of free jet before impingement. At <span class="choice-num">6 d</span> the pair sits <span class="choice-num">10.8 mm</span> apart on the face and the streams meet <span class="choice-num">9.2 mm</span> below it, close enough that the jets are still coherent.</li>
<li><span class="choice-title">Leave room for the wall, the film and the igniter</span>Out of a <span class="choice-num">56.8 mm</span> chamber radius, <span class="choice-num">6.8 mm</span> goes to the film-cooling ring and its walls and <span class="choice-num">12.5 mm</span> to the ASI bore at the center. The doublets get the <span class="choice-num">37.5 mm</span> band left over and impinge on its midline.</li>
<li><span class="choice-title">Manifolds five times the orifice</span>Each manifold passage is sized at 5× the area of the orifice it feeds (<span class="choice-num">15.3 mm²</span> ox, <span class="choice-num">9.5 mm²</span> fuel), so velocity in the manifold stays low and every orifice sees nearly the same pressure.</li>
</ul>

## Vendor review

The plate is printed in Inconel by an outside vendor, so before
anything was built the design went through their manufacturability
review. It came back with required changes. The problems were overhangs
and trapped powder: internal passages and manifold roofs that would have
needed support material inside the part, and enclosed cavities where
unfused powder would have had no way out after the build.

Neither problem shows up in a flow calculation or a clean CAD model.
Support material inside a 2 mm feed passage can't be reached to remove
it, and powder left in a manifold is debris headed straight for the
orifices. The review made it clear that the printer had to be a design
constraint from the first sketch, not a check at the end.

## Redesigning for additive manufacturing

Rather than patching the features the vendor flagged one at a time, we
went back through the whole plate and redesigned it for the process.
Taipan's injector is a separate plate and manifold; on this one the
face and both manifolds are a single print. That consolidation only
works if every internal feature can build without help:

- **45° overhang limit on every internal surface.** Anything flatter than 45° from the build plate needs supports, so no manifold roof, passage ceiling or bore is allowed past it. In the sections below, that is why the manifolds are triangles and cones rather than flat-roofed chambers.
- **Gothic-arch fuel passages.** A round or flat-topped passage has a section of roof that overhangs past 45°. A pointed arch never does, and it keeps more flow area for the hot kerosene coming out of the regen channels.
- **No enclosed cavities.** Every internal volume needs a path for unfused powder to leave after the build.
- **A floor on hole size.** The calc sheet carries a 0.8 mm minimum orifice diameter so nothing is drawn smaller than the printer can hold open.
- **Topology optimization against the printer, not just the loads.** The optimization pass below runs with the same 45° rule as a manufacturing constraint.

## Topological optimization

Once the injector's flow geometry was locked in, I ran it through
topological optimization to address manufacturability and mass:

- Defined the load cases directly from the combustion chamber sizing math (pressure and thermal-gradient estimates at the injector face) so the optimizer was solving against the environment the plate would actually see, not a generic load case.
- Optimized the plate's structure for 3D printability, removing material that wasn't load-bearing while keeping the part buildable within the same 45°-overhang constraint as the rest of the design.
- Ran the process multiple times, iterating between the optimizer's output and the printer's manufacturing constraints. A mathematically optimal shape isn't useful if it can't be printed reliably, so each pass fed back into the next. I kept iterating until the resulting mass distribution matched what the pressures and temperatures from the chamber analysis actually called for, not just what the optimizer converged on first.
